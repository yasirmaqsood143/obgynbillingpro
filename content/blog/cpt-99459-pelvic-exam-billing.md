For decades, the cost of a pelvic examination — the chaperone's time, the speculum, the supplies — was simply absorbed into the office visit. Since **January 1, 2024**, CPT has included an add-on code that recognizes those resources: **`+99459`, pelvic examination**.

The code is small in value but frequent in OB/GYN practices, which makes it worth getting right. Here's what it covers, when it can be billed, the documentation that supports it, and the mistakes we see most often.

## What 99459 represents

`99459` is an **add-on code** (marked with a `+` in CPT) reported in addition to a primary visit code when a pelvic examination is performed. The code's value comes entirely from **practice expense** — the clinical staff time involved in chaperoning and assisting, and the supply pack used for the exam (including a speculum). It carries **no physician work RVUs**, because the physician's work of performing the exam is already part of the E/M or preventive visit.

That distinction matters: `99459` isn't a way to bill more for the physician's exam. It recognizes resources the practice spends that weren't previously captured.

## Which codes 99459 is reported with

`99459` can be reported only with these primary services:

| Primary service | Codes |
|---|---|
| Office/outpatient E/M, new | `99202`–`99205` |
| Office/outpatient E/M, established | `99211`–`99215` |
| Office or other outpatient consultation | `99242`–`99245` |
| Preventive medicine, new patient | `99383`–`99387` |
| Preventive medicine, established patient | `99393`–`99397` |

It's reported on its own claim line, linked to the same diagnosis as the visit. It's not reported alone, and it's not reported with procedure codes that already include a pelvic exam.

## When to bill 99459

Bill `99459` when a pelvic examination is actually performed and documented during one of the eligible visits. Common OB/GYN examples:

- An annual well-woman visit (`99395`, for example) that includes a pelvic exam
- A problem visit for abnormal bleeding, pelvic pain or vaginal discharge in which a pelvic exam is performed
- A new patient consultation for a gynecologic concern that includes a pelvic exam

## When not to bill 99459

- **When no pelvic exam was performed.** A visit that includes only a breast exam or an external inspection doesn't qualify.
- **With Medicare's `G0101`.** That HCPCS code describes a screening pelvic and clinical breast exam, so the pelvic exam is already captured.
- **With procedures that include the exam.** Colposcopy, endometrial biopsy, IUD insertion and similar procedures include the examination necessary to perform them. If a separately identifiable E/M visit is billed with modifier `25` on the same day, evaluate carefully whether a separate pelvic exam was performed as part of that E/M.
- **During routine prenatal visits inside a global package.** Through 2026, routine antepartum visits are part of the global obstetric fee and aren't billed separately, so there's nothing for `99459` to attach to. (Problem visits billed separately during pregnancy are a different matter.)

## Documentation requirements

Because `99459` reflects staff time and supplies, the documentation should show that the pelvic exam was actually performed. Good documentation includes:

- **The pelvic examination findings** in the physical exam section — external genitalia, vagina, cervix, uterus, adnexa as appropriate
- **Chaperone documentation** — the name or role of the chaperone present, or that a chaperone was offered and declined
- **The reason for the exam** if it's a problem-oriented visit

Templated "normal pelvic exam" text without any individualized findings is weak support, particularly if it appears in every note regardless of visit type.

## Payment and coverage

Payer adoption of `99459` has been uneven since 2024:

- Some commercial plans pay it separately, at a modest rate reflecting its practice-expense value.
- Some payers treat it as **bundled** into the visit and deny it as included (often with a `CO-97` adjustment).
- Some Medicaid programs haven't added it to their fee schedules.

Review each major payer's published policy and your remits. If a payer bundles it, keep billing it only if the payer asks for it for data purposes; otherwise it just creates denial noise in your A/R. Your billing system should have a payer-specific rule either way.

## Preventive visits, problem visits and 99459

A common OB/GYN scenario: a patient's annual preventive visit also addresses a new problem — for example pelvic pain — and a pelvic exam is performed.

- The **preventive visit** (`99395`, for example) is billed.
- A **problem-oriented E/M** may also be billed with modifier `25` if the problem required significant, separately identifiable work.
- **`99459`** is reported once, for the pelvic exam.

There's only one pelvic exam, so `99459` is reported once per encounter regardless of how many visit codes appear on the claim.

## How 99459 fits with Medicare screening codes

For Medicare patients, screening pelvic exams are billed with:

- `G0101` — cervical or vaginal cancer screening; pelvic and clinical breast examination
- `Q0091` — screening Papanicolaou smear; obtaining, preparing and conveyance of specimen to laboratory

These have their own frequency rules (generally every 24 months, or every 12 months for patients at high risk). When `G0101` is billed, `99459` doesn't add anything. When a Medicare patient has a **problem-oriented** visit with a medically necessary pelvic exam, `99459` may apply with the E/M visit, depending on Medicare's current payment status for the code. Check the current Medicare Physician Fee Schedule status before billing.

## Common 99459 billing mistakes

1. **Billing it without a pelvic exam documented** — especially from templated charge forms that add it automatically
2. **Billing it with `G0101`** on Medicare claims
3. **Billing it more than once** on a claim with both preventive and problem visits
4. **Billing it with procedures** that include the exam
5. **Ignoring payer bundling**, which fills A/R with small denials nobody works
6. **Missing it entirely** for payers that do pay — small amounts, but frequent

## Should your practice bill 99459?

For most OB/GYN practices, yes — where payers recognize it and the documentation supports it. Individually it's a small amount, but OB/GYN practices perform pelvic exams many times a day, and the code exists precisely to recognize the staff time and supplies those exams require.

The key is building it into your workflow properly: charge capture tied to documented exams, payer-specific rules for bundling, and periodic review to make sure it's being used correctly.

## Setting up 99459 in your billing system

Because `99459` is low-dollar and high-volume, automation matters. A good setup looks like this:

- **Charge capture tied to documentation.** Instead of a superbill checkbox that's easy to tick by habit, link the charge to the pelvic exam section of the note or an exam-performed flag.
- **Payer-specific rules.** For payers that pay `99459`, the charge goes out. For payers that bundle it, either suppress it or bill it with an expected zero payment so it doesn't clutter denial work queues.
- **Edits against `G0101`.** Prevent `99459` from going out on the same claim as `G0101`.
- **One per encounter.** An edit that blocks a second `99459` on the same date of service.
- **Linkage to the right visit.** Point the add-on to the diagnosis that explains why the pelvic exam was performed.

## A note on chaperones

The value of `99459` reflects chaperone time, and many professional societies and state medical boards address chaperone use during sensitive examinations. Even apart from billing, documenting the chaperone's presence — or the patient's informed decision to decline one — is good practice. It also happens to be the documentation that best supports `99459`.
