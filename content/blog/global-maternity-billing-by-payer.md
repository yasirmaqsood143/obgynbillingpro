Every payer uses the same CPT maternity codes — `59400`, `59510`, `59425`, `59430` and the rest (through 2026). But how payers **apply** those codes varies enough that a claim built perfectly for one plan can deny at another. For obstetric practices with a mix of Medicaid, managed care and commercial patients, payer-specific rules are often the difference between a clean maternity revenue cycle and a frustrating one.

This article explains what varies by payer type and how to build a simple payer rule table. Because policies change, always confirm against each payer's current provider manual.

## What varies from payer to payer

| Area | Examples of how payers differ |
|---|---|
| **Billing timing** | After delivery; after the postpartum visit; or visit-by-visit for antepartum care |
| **Date of service** | Delivery date; date span from first visit to delivery; or postpartum visit date |
| **Notification** | Pregnancy notification or first-visit claim required early in pregnancy — or not |
| **Modifiers** | `TH` required on prenatal/postpartum visits; specific modifiers for twins |
| **Insurance changes** | Components required, or global accepted with limited date span |
| **Complication visits** | Which E/M visits during pregnancy are separately payable |
| **Ultrasound limits** | Number of routine obstetric ultrasounds covered; required diagnoses |
| **Multiple gestations** | Second delivery code with `51`/`59`, or modifier `22` on one code |
| **Assistant at cesarean** | Whether an assistant is paid |
| **Timely filing** | From date of service or delivery; 90 days to a year or more |

## Medicaid

Medicaid pays for a large share of births in the United States, and every state runs its program differently. Common features:

- **Fee-for-service and managed care.** Most states deliver Medicaid through managed care organizations (MCOs). The state sets a baseline, but each MCO has its own claims rules, authorization requirements and appeal process. A practice may work with several MCOs in the same state.
- **Pregnancy notification.** Many Medicaid programs and MCOs ask for a pregnancy notification or risk assessment early in prenatal care — sometimes with incentive payments for timely first-trimester visits.
- **Visit-by-visit billing.** Some programs want antepartum visits billed as they occur rather than in a package.
- **`TH` modifier.** Frequently required on prenatal and postpartum services. See our [TH modifier guide](/blog/th-modifier-obstetric-billing).
- **Presumptive eligibility.** Patients may be covered while a full application is processed, with specific billing rules for that period.
- **Extended postpartum coverage.** Most states now cover postpartum care for 12 months, which affects billing for postpartum visits after the global period.

## Blue Cross Blue Shield plans

Blue Cross Blue Shield isn't one company — it's a federation of independent plans, each licensed for a specific area. That means **there's no single "BCBS global maternity policy."** Each plan publishes its own:

- Maternity reimbursement policy and billing timing
- Rules for split care and insurance changes
- Ultrasound and prenatal testing policies
- Modifier and multiple-gestation rules

Plans also differ between their commercial, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid lines of business. When a patient has an out-of-area BCBS plan, claims are generally submitted to the local plan, but the patient's home plan's benefits apply.

## Other commercial payers

National commercial payers each publish maternity or obstetric reimbursement policies, usually in a policy library on their provider websites. Look for:

- Global obstetric care policy
- Obstetric ultrasound policy
- Multiple procedure and multiple gestation policy
- Assistant surgeon policy
- Telehealth policy (for virtual prenatal visits)

Self-funded employer plans administered by a commercial payer may have plan-specific benefits — particularly for fertility and out-of-network care — even when the payer's general reimbursement policies apply.

## TRICARE

Military families are covered by TRICARE, which publishes its own maternity coverage and billing guidance through its regional contractors. Like other payers, it uses the CPT maternity codes, with TRICARE-specific rules on authorization, network status and billing.

## Medicare

Medicare covers relatively few pregnancies — typically patients eligible through disability or end-stage renal disease. When it applies, Medicare's physician fee schedule lists the maternity codes with the global indicator `MMM`, and the CPT maternity guidelines define what's included.

## Out-of-network maternity care

When a patient's plan doesn't include your practice in-network:

- **Verify out-of-network benefits** at the first visit — deductibles, coinsurance and out-of-pocket maximums can be very different.
- **Document financial counseling** so the patient understands potential costs.
- **Check balance-billing protections** — federal and state laws restrict balance billing in certain situations, especially emergency care and some services at in-network facilities.
- **Consider single-case agreements** for patients with ongoing care needs, where payers allow them.

## Getting ready for 2027 payer policies

The CPT 2027 maternity changes delete the global package codes for dates of service on or after January 1, 2027. Every payer will need new maternity policies covering per-encounter prenatal billing, labor management and delivery codes, postpartum visits, cost-sharing and transition rules for pregnancies that span the change. Watch your payers' bulletins closely this winter and update your rule table as policies arrive. Our [2027 maternity transition guide](/resources/2027-maternity-transition) covers what's changing.
