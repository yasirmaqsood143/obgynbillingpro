Billing and coding staff see more protected health information (PHI) than almost anyone else in an OB/GYN practice: diagnoses, procedures, pregnancy status, insurance details and payment histories for every patient. HIPAA applies to all of it. Women's health adds sensitivity — pregnancy, fertility treatment, pregnancy loss, sexually transmitted infections and other services patients may not want shared, even with family members on the same insurance policy.

These are the HIPAA essentials every OB/GYN billing team should know.

*This article is educational and not legal advice. Consult your compliance officer or healthcare attorney for guidance on your specific situation.*

## Minimum necessary access

HIPAA's **minimum necessary** standard requires using, disclosing and requesting only the PHI needed for the task. For billing staff:

- **Role-based access** in the PM/EHR — billers don't need access to every clinical note
- **Individual user accounts** — never shared logins
- **Access removed promptly** when someone changes roles or leaves
- **Only the needed records** sent with appeals — not entire charts
- **No browsing** — looking up a neighbor's or celebrity's account is a violation

## Secure handling of claims, EOBs and remittances

- Store paper EOBs and correspondence securely and shred when no longer needed
- Don't leave PHI on printers, desks or screens
- Use **secure portals** and encrypted channels for transmitting records
- Verify fax numbers and use cover sheets
- Lock screens when stepping away; use automatic timeouts

## Communicating with patients

- **Verify identity** before discussing an account — name plus at least one other identifier
- Follow the patient's **communication preferences** and any confidential communication requests
- Be careful with **voicemails** — leave minimal information
- **Statements** go to the address on file; patients may request alternative addresses for sensitive services

### Sensitive services and family plans

When a patient is a dependent on someone else's insurance, explanation-of-benefits statements may go to the policyholder. Some states have laws allowing confidential communications for sensitive services. Billing staff should know the practice's procedures for patients who request confidentiality, and route those questions appropriately.

## Messaging apps and email

- **Don't send PHI** through personal email, text messages or consumer messaging apps unless the practice has approved a secure method and the patient has agreed as required
- At OBGYNBillingPro, **WhatsApp is used for general questions and scheduling only** — never for patient information
- Use encrypted email or secure portals for any PHI that must be transmitted

## Business associates

Every vendor that creates, receives, maintains or transmits PHI on the practice's behalf — billing companies, clearinghouses, EHR vendors, collection agencies, shredding services — is a business associate and must sign a **Business Associate Agreement** before receiving PHI. Billing staff should know which vendors are approved and never send PHI to a vendor without a BAA in place.

## Coding accuracy and compliance overlap

Accurate coding is a compliance issue, not just a revenue issue. Submitting claims for services not documented, upcoding, or unbundling to increase payment can create liability under federal and state laws. Billing staff should:

- Code only what's documented
- Ask providers when documentation is unclear — never assume
- Report concerns through the practice's compliance process
- Never alter documentation to support a claim

Regular [coding audits](/coding-audits) are part of an effective compliance program.

## Breaches and incident reporting

Billing staff should know:

- **What counts as a potential breach:** misdirected statements, lost laptops or paper, emails sent to the wrong person, unauthorized access
- **Report immediately** to the practice's privacy officer — don't try to handle it alone
- The practice evaluates whether notification is required under the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule

## Training should be role-specific

Generic annual HIPAA videos aren't enough for billing staff. Effective training covers:

- Real scenarios: a spouse calling about a bill, a misdirected EOB, a request from an attorney
- Sensitive services in women's health
- The practice's specific tools: secure email, portals, approved messaging
- How to recognize and report incidents
- Phishing and social engineering — billing staff are frequent targets

Document training and refresh it at least annually and when procedures change.

## Watching for regulatory changes

HHS has proposed significant updates to the HIPAA Security Rule, including more specific requirements for risk analysis, multi-factor authentication and encryption. As of this writing, the timing of a final rule is uncertain. Practices should follow HHS announcements and review security practices with their IT and compliance advisors — many of the proposed safeguards are good practice regardless.

## Key takeaways

- Billing staff handle more PHI than most roles — minimum necessary applies.
- Verify identity and respect confidentiality requests, especially for sensitive services.
- Never send PHI through unapproved channels.
- BAAs are required before any vendor receives PHI.
- Accurate coding is part of compliance.

## Quick reference: can I share this?

| Request | Usual approach |
|---|---|
| Patient calls about her own bill | Verify identity, then discuss |
| Spouse calls about the patient's bill | Only if the patient has agreed or the spouse is a personal representative or responsible party per practice policy |
| Insurance company requests records for a claim | Permitted for payment; send only what's needed |
| Attorney requests records | Requires valid authorization or legal process — route to the privacy officer |
| Collection agency (with BAA) needs account details | Minimum necessary for collection |
| Another provider requests records for treatment | Generally permitted; follow practice procedures |

When in doubt, pause and ask the privacy officer.
