"How much does it cost?" is the first question most practice owners ask about outsourcing billing — and the hardest to answer from a website. Pricing depends on your volume, specialty mix, payer mix, the services you need and how much existing A/R needs work. What we *can* do is explain how pricing works, what should be included, which fees to watch for and how to compare outsourcing with keeping billing in-house.

## The common pricing models

### 1. Percentage of collections

The most common model. The billing company earns a percentage of the money collected on the claims it handles.

**Pros:** Incentives are aligned — the company earns more when you collect more. Costs scale with revenue, which helps new or seasonal practices.

**Cons:** Monthly cost varies. Ask what "collections" means: insurance payments only, or patient payments too? Are payments on claims submitted before the contract included?

**What affects the percentage:** practice volume (higher volume usually means a lower percentage), specialty complexity, payer mix (heavy Medicaid managed care often takes more follow-up), and scope (full RCM vs. claims only).

### 2. Flat monthly fee

A fixed monthly price, usually based on provider count, claim volume or both.

**Pros:** Predictable budgeting.

**Cons:** Less direct incentive to chase difficult claims. Make sure the contract defines volume limits and what happens if volume grows.

### 3. Per-claim fee

A fixed fee for each claim submitted.

**Pros:** Simple, transparent for high-volume, low-complexity billing.

**Cons:** Rewards claim volume rather than collections. Denial work and appeals may be extra. Usually a poor fit for OB/GYN, where much of the value lies in follow-up.

### 4. Hybrid models

A smaller base fee plus a lower percentage, or different rates for different services (for example, a percentage for ongoing billing and a separate fee for credentialing or old A/R cleanup).

## What should be included

Get a written list. For full-service OB/GYN billing, the fee should normally cover:

- Charge entry and coding review (or coding, if you outsource coding too)
- Claim scrubbing and electronic submission
- Rejection handling at the clearinghouse
- Payment posting (electronic and paper)
- Denial management and appeals — **every** denial, not just easy ones
- A/R follow-up on unpaid claims
- Patient statements and handling patient billing questions
- Monthly reporting and a review call
- OB-specific work: pregnancy tracking, split-care billing, delivery claim review

Commonly **separate** (and that's fine, as long as it's clear):

- Provider credentialing and enrollment
- Cleanup of old A/R from before the engagement
- Comprehensive coding audits
- Fee schedule and contract analysis
- New practice setup

## Hidden costs to watch for

- **Setup or onboarding fees** — some companies charge them; we don't.
- **Clearinghouse and statement fees** passed through at a markup.
- **Software fees** if the company requires its own platform.
- **Minimum monthly fees** that apply even at low volume.
- **Long contracts with termination penalties.**
- **Fees on payments you collected yourself**, such as patient payments at the front desk.
- **Charges for reports**, records requests or data export when you leave.

Ask for a sample invoice. It reveals more than a proposal.

## Comparing outsourcing to in-house billing

To compare fairly, total up the annual cost of in-house billing:

| In-house cost | What to include |
|---|---|
| Salaries | Billers, coders, billing manager |
| Benefits and payroll taxes | Health insurance, retirement, PTO |
| Software | PM/billing module, coding tools, add-ons |
| Clearinghouse | Per-claim or monthly fees, ERA/EFT, statements |
| Training | Coding updates, certifications, conferences |
| Turnover | Recruiting, onboarding, lost productivity during vacancies |
| Management time | Physician or administrator time supervising billing |
| Space and equipment | Workstations, office space |

Then compare it with the outsourcing fee **and** the likely effect on collections. A cheaper option that collects less isn't cheaper.

## A simple example (illustrative)

Suppose a practice collects $1,000,000 a year. At a hypothetical 6% of collections, outsourcing would cost $60,000. If the in-house alternative is one full-time biller plus benefits, software and clearinghouse fees, the total may be similar or higher — before accounting for vacancy coverage or specialty expertise. If outsourcing also improves collections even modestly, the comparison shifts further. If your in-house team is experienced and stable, the comparison may favor keeping billing in-house.

*These figures are illustrative only and are not a quote. Your numbers will differ.*

## Questions to ask before signing

1. What's your OB/GYN-specific experience — global maternity, GYN surgery, the 2027 transition?
2. What exactly is included in the fee? What costs extra?
3. How is "collections" defined for the percentage?
4. Will you work in our existing EHR/PM system?
5. Who is our point of contact, and how quickly do you respond?
6. What will monthly reports show?
7. How are denials worked and tracked?
8. What's the contract length and termination process? Do we keep our data?
9. Will you sign a BAA?
10. Can we start with a free audit to see your work?
