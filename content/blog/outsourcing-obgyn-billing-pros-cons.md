Outsourcing billing is one of the biggest operational decisions an OB/GYN practice makes. Done well, it brings specialized expertise and steadier cash flow. Done poorly — or with the wrong partner — it creates frustration and lost revenue. This article lays out the honest trade-offs, how to calculate whether it makes financial sense, and how to choose and transition if you decide to go ahead.

## The case for in-house billing

In-house billing works well for many practices. Its real advantages:

- **Direct control.** Billing staff are down the hall and report to you.
- **Immediate communication** with providers and front-desk staff.
- **Institutional knowledge** — an experienced biller knows your providers, payers and patients.
- **Flexibility** to reprioritize work instantly.

It works best when you have **experienced, stable billing staff** with OB/GYN knowledge, enough volume to justify specialization, and management capacity to supervise billing.

## The risks of in-house billing

- **Key-person dependency.** When your one experienced biller leaves or takes leave, billing slows or stops.
- **Specialty knowledge gaps.** Global maternity rules, surgical coding, payer policies and the 2027 transition require ongoing education.
- **Hidden costs** — benefits, software, clearinghouse fees, training and turnover.
- **Limited bandwidth** for denials and aged A/R when daily work fills the day.

## The case for outsourcing

- **Specialized expertise.** A team that bills OB/GYN every day knows the rules and the payers.
- **Coverage.** Vacations and turnover don't stop billing.
- **Process.** Denials, A/R follow-up and reporting are handled systematically.
- **Scalability.** The service grows with your practice.
- **Less management burden** on physicians and administrators.

## The risks of outsourcing

- **Less direct control.** You depend on the vendor's communication and reporting.
- **Choosing the wrong partner.** A generalist company may not know global maternity or GYN surgery coding.
- **Transition disruption** if the cutover isn't planned.
- **Misaligned incentives** if pricing rewards volume rather than collections.

## Calculating real ROI

Compare full costs, not just salaries.

**In-house annual cost:**
- Salaries for billers, coders and a billing manager
- Benefits and payroll taxes
- PM/billing software and add-ons
- Clearinghouse, ERA/EFT and statement fees
- Training and coding resources
- Turnover: recruiting, onboarding, lost productivity during vacancies
- Physician and administrator time spent managing billing

**Outsourced annual cost:**
- The billing company's fee (commonly a percentage of collections)
- Any services not included
- Internal staff time still required (front desk, charge entry, provider documentation)

**Then estimate the effect on collections.** This is the part most comparisons skip. If outsourcing improves your net collection rate, reduces denials or recovers aged A/R, the added collections can outweigh the fee. If your in-house team is already performing well, the math may favor staying in-house. A free audit of your claims and A/R gives you real numbers instead of guesses. ## A middle ground: hybrid models

You don't have to choose all or nothing. Common hybrid setups:

| Keep in-house | Outsource |
|---|---|
| Scheduling, registration, front-desk collections | Denial management and appeals |
| Charge entry | Aged A/R recovery |
| Patient phone calls | Coding review or audits |
| Daily claim submission | Credentialing and enrollment |

Hybrid models let you keep what's working and get specialist help where you need it most.

## Red flags when choosing a billing company

- No specific OB/GYN experience — or vague answers about global maternity and the 2027 changes
- Guaranteed results before seeing your data
- Unclear pricing, or essential work (appeals, follow-up) charged separately
- Long contracts with heavy termination penalties
- Reports that show activity ("claims submitted") but not performance
- Reluctance to sign a BAA or to work inside your systems
- No named point of contact

## Questions to ask

1. How many OB/GYN practices do you work with, and for how long?
2. How do you handle split-care pregnancies and insurance changes?
3. How are you preparing clients for the 2027 maternity codes?
4. What's included in your fee?
5. What will monthly reports show?
6. Who will be our point of contact?
7. What happens if we leave?

## Transition checklist

If you decide to outsource:

1. Sign the **BAA** and the service agreement.
2. Set up **individual user accounts** in your PM system and clearinghouse.
3. Document **payers, fee schedules, authorizations** and current workflows.
4. Agree on a **cutover date** for new claims.
5. Decide who works **existing A/R** — the new partner, your staff or both.
6. Set **communication channels** and a monthly review meeting.
7. Watch **rejections and denials closely** for the first 90 days.

## Key takeaways

- In-house billing works well with experienced, stable staff and good management.
- Outsourcing brings expertise and coverage, but partner choice is critical.
- Compare full costs **and** the effect on collections.
- Hybrid models are often the best fit.

## What good outsourcing looks like after 90 days

If you do outsource, here's what you should be able to see by the end of the first quarter:

- Claims going out daily with a stable or improving first-pass acceptance rate
- Every denial categorized, with the top causes identified and fixes under way
- Aged A/R being worked in order of value and deadline
- A monthly report you understand, and a regular review call
- Fewer billing questions landing on physicians' desks

If you can't see these, ask why — and expect a clear answer.
