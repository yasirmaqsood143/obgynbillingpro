"How long will credentialing take?" is the question every new provider and practice manager asks — and the honest answer is "it depends." But it depends on predictable things. Understanding them lets you plan a start date that doesn't leave a new physician seeing patients with no way to bill.

## Why official timelines are optimistic

Payers often quote processing times that assume a **complete, clean application** submitted to a network that's **open**, with **every supporting document current**. In practice, applications are frequently returned for missing information, CAQH profiles aren't authorized for the payer, or the payer's queue is longer than usual. Each round trip adds weeks.

The clock also doesn't start when you think it does. Many payers count from the date the application is complete — not the date you first submitted it.

## Typical timelines by payer type

These ranges reflect common experience, not guarantees:

| Payer type | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Medicare (PECOS) | Several weeks to a few months | Faster when applications are complete; Medicare has rules for retrospective billing in limited circumstances |
| State Medicaid | Several weeks to several months | Varies widely by state; may require site visits or fingerprinting for some provider types |
| Medicaid managed care plans | 60–120+ days | Separate from state Medicaid enrollment in many states |
| Large commercial payers | 60–120+ days | Contracting (if new to the payer) adds time beyond credentialing |
| Regional/smaller plans | 30–120+ days | Highly variable |
| Re-credentialing | Shorter, but deadlines are firm | Missing a re-credentialing deadline can cause termination |

Plan for the upper end of these ranges, and treat anything faster as a bonus.

## Credentialing vs. contracting

Two different processes are often confused:

- **Credentialing** verifies the provider's qualifications.
- **Contracting** establishes the agreement and fee schedule between the payer and the practice.

When a provider joins a practice that already has a contract, the provider is typically **added** to the existing contract after credentialing — faster. When the practice is new to the payer, contracting must happen too, which can add significant time.

## What actually causes delays

1. **Incomplete applications** — the number one cause.
2. **CAQH problems** — profile not attested, documents expired, or payer not authorized to view it.
3. **Work history gaps** over six months without explanation.
4. **Malpractice documentation** that doesn't match the application.
5. **Slow responses** to payer requests for information.
6. **Closed networks** in areas the payer considers adequately served.
7. **Address or taxonomy mismatches** between NPPES, CAQH and the application.
8. **Missing hospital affiliation** for delivering providers.

## What actually speeds things up

- **Start early** — the moment the contract is signed.
- **Submit complete applications** the first time; use a checklist. See our [credentialing checklist for new OB/GYN providers](/blog/credentialing-checklist-new-obgyn).
- **Keep CAQH current** and authorize every payer.
- **Follow up every one to two weeks** and log each contact.
- **Respond to requests within days.**
- **Escalate** stalled applications through provider relations.
- **Use online portals** where payers offer them — they're usually faster than paper or fax.

## Planning around realistic timelines

| Weeks before start date | Action |
|---|---|
| 16–24 | Gather documents, set up NPI/taxonomy, update CAQH |
| 12–20 | Submit Medicare, Medicaid and top commercial applications |
| 8–16 | Follow up; submit remaining payers; hospital privileges in process |
| 4–8 | Confirm effective dates as they come in; share with billing and scheduling |
| Start date | Schedule with patients whose plans are effective; hold or plan around pending payers |

If the start date can't move, plan the schedule around enrollment status rather than billing under another provider's NPI — a practice that creates compliance risk.

## What about "rapid" or expedited credentialing?

Some payers offer expedited processes in specific circumstances — for example, adding a provider to an existing group contract, or provisional credentialing for certain provider types. These vary by payer and state; some states have laws requiring payers to process complete applications within a set time. Ask each payer's provider relations team what options exist, and get the answer in writing.

## Re-credentialing and revalidation

Credentialing isn't one-and-done:

- **Commercial payers** re-credential providers on a cycle (often every two to three years).
- **Medicare** revalidates enrollment on its own schedule.
- **CAQH** requires re-attestation every 120 days.

Missing these deadlines can terminate participation — and getting reinstated can take as long as initial credentialing. Track every date.

## Key takeaways

- Expect months, not weeks, for commercial credentialing.
- Most delays come from incomplete applications and slow follow-up.
- Start as early as possible and follow up relentlessly.
- Plan the provider's schedule around effective dates.

## Credentialing status tracker

Keep one simple tracker per provider. For each payer, record:

- Application type (initial, add to group, re-credentialing)
- Submission date and method (portal, email, CAQH)
- Reference or case number
- Last follow-up date and outcome
- Next follow-up date
- Outstanding requests for information
- Effective date, provider ID and contract link once approved

Review it weekly. A tracker turns credentialing from a black box into a list of next actions — and makes it obvious which payers need escalation.
