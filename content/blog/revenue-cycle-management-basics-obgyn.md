Revenue cycle management — RCM — is a term that gets used for everything from billing software to outsourcing contracts. At its core, it simply means **the full process of turning patient care into collected revenue**. For OB/GYN practices, that process has a few extra loops that make it longer and more complex than most specialties'.

This guide walks through each stage of the OB/GYN revenue cycle, what can go wrong at each step, and how to keep money flowing.

## The stages of the revenue cycle

### 1. Scheduling and registration

Revenue starts with accurate patient information: legal name, date of birth, address, insurance carrier, member ID, subscriber details and secondary coverage. Small errors here — a transposed digit in a member ID, a missing secondary policy — become denials weeks later.

**OB/GYN specifics:** for obstetric patients, capture whether they had prenatal care elsewhere, the estimated due date, and whether insurance is likely to change (new job, marriage, Medicaid application in progress).

### 2. Eligibility, benefits and authorization

Verify coverage before the visit and confirm benefits for the services planned — preventive vs. diagnostic, maternity benefits, ultrasound limits, surgical coverage. Obtain prior authorization where required, matched to the planned CPT codes.

**OB/GYN specifics:** re-verify coverage during pregnancy, and secure authorizations for hysterectomy, myomectomy, some imaging and fertility services. See [eligibility verification](/eligibility-verification).

### 3. Documentation and charge capture

The provider documents the encounter; documented services become charges. Charge lag — the time between service and charge entry — delays everything downstream.

**OB/GYN specifics:** ultrasounds, non-stress tests, LARC devices and their J-codes, pelvic exam add-ons and complication visits during pregnancy are commonly missed. Surgical charges should wait for pathology when uterine weight or diagnosis affects coding.

### 4. Coding

Services are translated into CPT/HCPCS procedure codes and ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes, with modifiers as needed.

**OB/GYN specifics:** global maternity package vs. components, Chapter 15 diagnosis sequencing, hysterectomy weight tiers, NCCI bundling in gynecologic surgery, and modifier `25` on preventive-plus-problem visits. See [coding audits](/coding-audits).

### 5. Claim scrubbing and submission

Claims are checked for errors before submission — invalid codes, missing data, NCCI conflicts, payer-specific rules — then sent electronically through a clearinghouse. Rejections at the clearinghouse should be fixed the same day.

### 6. Payment posting and reconciliation

Payments from electronic remittance advice (ERA) are posted to patient accounts and reconciled to bank deposits. Payments should be compared to contracted rates to catch underpayments.

### 7. Denial management

Denied claims are corrected, sent for reconsideration or appealed, and every denial is categorized by root cause so upstream problems get fixed. See [denial management](/denial-management).

### 8. Accounts receivable follow-up

Unpaid claims are followed up by age and value before timely-filing limits expire. Aged A/R is reviewed regularly. See [how to reduce days in A/R](/blog/how-to-reduce-days-in-ar).

### 9. Patient billing and collections

Patient responsibility — copays, deductibles, coinsurance — is collected at the visit where possible, then billed with clear statements and payment options.

### 10. Reporting and improvement

Key metrics — net collection rate, days in A/R, denial rate by reason, charge lag — are tracked monthly so problems are visible early. See [OB/GYN billing KPIs](/blog/obgyn-billing-kpis).

## Why OB/GYN revenue cycles run longer

- **Global maternity billing delays obstetric revenue.** Through 2026, a pregnancy's routine care is typically billed once, after delivery — months after the first visit.
- **Surgical claims wait for documentation.** Operative notes and pathology reports can take days or weeks.
- **Coverage changes during pregnancy** create rework when they're caught late.
- **Medicaid managed care** often involves more follow-up than commercial plans.

That's why standard A/R reports can look healthy while obstetric revenue sits unbilled. Tracking open pregnancies and unbilled deliveries is essential.

## Where OB/GYN practices typically lose the most

1. **Front-end errors** — eligibility and authorization problems that cause denials no coding can fix
2. **Unbilled obstetric care** after transfers, losses or insurance changes
3. **Unworked denials** — especially small ones that never get touched
4. **Undercoded surgery** — billed before pathology, missing adnexal procedures
5. **Missed charges** — ultrasounds, devices, add-on codes
6. **Underpayments** — payments below contracted rates that look "paid"
7. **Uncollected patient balances** — no collection at the visit, unclear statements

## In-house, outsourced or hybrid?

Practices run their revenue cycle in three ways:

- **In-house:** your own staff using your PM system. Works well with experienced, stable billing staff.
- **Outsourced:** a billing company runs the revenue cycle, usually inside your systems. Works well when specialized staff are hard to hire or retain.
- **Hybrid:** in-house front desk and charge entry, outsourced denials, A/R or coding. Common and effective.

See our comparison of [RCM software and outsourced billing](/blog/obgyn-rcm-software-vs-outsourced-billing) and [the pros and cons of outsourcing](/blog/outsourcing-obgyn-billing-pros-cons).

## Preparing the revenue cycle for 2027

The global maternity package ends January 1, 2027. Prenatal and postpartum visits will be billed per encounter, labor and delivery with new codes. Every stage of the revenue cycle — registration, eligibility, charge capture, coding, claims and patient billing — needs updates. Start with the [2027 maternity transition guide](/resources/2027-maternity-transition).

## Key takeaways

- The revenue cycle starts at scheduling, not at claim submission.
- OB/GYN adds pregnancy tracking, surgical documentation and payer-specific obstetric rules.
- Most lost revenue comes from a handful of repeatable problems that can be fixed with process.
- Measure monthly, fix the biggest causes first.

## Who owns each stage?

| Stage | Typical owner |
|---|---|
| Scheduling and registration | Front desk |
| Eligibility and authorization | Front desk or verification team |
| Documentation | Providers |
| Charge capture and coding | Providers, coders |
| Claims and rejections | Billing team |
| Payment posting | Billing team |
| Denials and A/R | Billing team or billing partner |
| Patient billing | Billing team, front desk |
| Reporting | Practice manager, billing partner |

When everyone knows which stage they own — and how it affects the next one — problems get fixed where they start.
