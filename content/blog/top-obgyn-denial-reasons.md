Denials are the most visible symptom of revenue cycle problems — and in OB/GYN, they cluster around a predictable set of causes. The good news: most are preventable once you know where they come from. Here are the nine denial reasons we see most often in OB/GYN billing, what causes each, and how to prevent them.

## 1. Eligibility and coverage problems

**Typical codes:** `CO-27` (coverage terminated), `CO-31` (patient not identified), `CO-22` (another payer primary), `CO-109` (not covered by this payer)

**Causes:** outdated insurance on file, coverage changes mid-pregnancy, wrong primary/secondary order, Medicaid plan assignment changes.

**Prevention:** verify eligibility before every visit, re-verify during pregnancy, confirm coordination of benefits with patients at check-in, and check Medicaid managed care assignment monthly for obstetric patients. See [eligibility verification](/eligibility-verification).

## 2. Missing or mismatched prior authorization

**Typical codes:** `CO-197` (precertification/authorization absent), `CO-198` (precertification exceeded)

**Causes:** no authorization requested, authorization for a different CPT code than billed, expired authorization, procedure changed in the OR.

**Prevention:** an authorization checklist by payer, authorization matched to planned CPT codes, and a process to update authorizations within the payer's window when the procedure changes. See [which OB/GYN procedures require prior authorization](/blog/prior-authorization-obgyn-procedures).

## 3. Bundling denials (CO-97)

**Typical code:** `CO-97` (service included in payment for another service)

**Causes:** routine visits billed during the global obstetric package, routine post-op visits billed in a surgical global period, NCCI code pairs without supporting modifiers, E/M with minor procedures without modifier `25`.

**Prevention:** global period tracking, NCCI edits in the claim scrubber, and modifiers applied only with documentation. Read our full [CO-97 denial code guide](/blog/co-97-denial-code-explained).

## 4. Diagnosis errors

**Typical codes:** `CO-11` (diagnosis inconsistent with procedure), `CO-16` (claim lacks information), `CO-146` (diagnosis invalid for date of service)

**Causes:** missing or wrong trimester characters, `Z34` used with O codes, missing `Z37` on delivery claims, `O80` misused, outdated codes after the October 1 ICD-10-CM update, non-pregnancy diagnoses on obstetric services.

**Prevention:** scrubber edits for Chapter 15 rules, annual code updates loaded on time, and provider templates that update diagnoses at each visit. See [multiple O codes sequencing](/blog/multiple-o-codes-sequencing-pregnancy).

## 5. Global period conflicts

**Typical codes:** `CO-97`, `CO-B15` (procedure qualifying service not paid or identified)

**Causes:** visits during surgical global periods without modifier `24`, procedures without `58`, `78` or `79`, prenatal visits billed outside the package without a complication diagnosis.

**Prevention:** global period flags in the PM system, `99024` for routine post-op visits, and clear documentation for unrelated problems.

## 6. Credentialing gaps

**Typical codes:** `CO-B7` (provider not certified/eligible for this procedure or date), out-of-network denials, `CO-242` (services not provided by network/primary care providers)

**Causes:** new providers billing before enrollment is effective, lapsed re-credentialing or CAQH attestation, new locations not added, taxonomy mismatches.

**Prevention:** an enrollment tracker by provider and payer, holding claims until effective dates are confirmed, and re-credentialing reminders. See [credentialing](/credentialing).

## 7. Timely filing

**Typical code:** `CO-29` (time limit for filing has expired)

**Causes:** global OB claims held too long after delivery, slow charge entry, claims rejected at the clearinghouse and never resubmitted, secondary claims not filed after primary payment.

**Prevention:** know each payer's limit, monitor claims with no response, fix rejections the same day, and keep proof of timely filing (clearinghouse acceptance reports).

## 8. Medical necessity

**Typical code:** `CO-50` (not deemed a medical necessity)

**Causes:** ultrasounds beyond payer frequency limits without high-risk indications, surgery without documentation that meets payer criteria, diagnosis codes that don't support the service.

**Prevention:** know payer medical policies for high-volume services (OB ultrasound, hysterectomy, infertility), link diagnoses accurately, and document indications. See our [OB ultrasound billing guide](/blog/ob-ultrasound-billing-cpt-76801-76817).

## 9. Duplicate claims

**Typical code:** `CO-18` (exact duplicate claim/service)

**Causes:** resubmitting instead of sending a corrected claim, or two providers billing the same global package.

**Prevention:** use corrected claims (frequency code `7`) or voids (frequency code `8`), and coordinate with other practices in split-care pregnancies.

## How to tell which denials matter most

Count denials **by reason and by dollar value** each month. A reason with few denials but high dollars (for example, surgical authorization denials) may deserve attention before a frequent, low-dollar reason. Then look for the upstream process behind the top two or three reasons and fix it. Track the trend to confirm the fix worked.

| Denial category | Upstream owner |
|---|---|
| Eligibility / COB | Front desk, verification team |
| Authorization | Surgical scheduler, verification team |
| Bundling / modifiers | Coding, providers |
| Diagnosis | Providers, coding |
| Credentialing | Credentialing coordinator |
| Timely filing | Billing team |
| Medical necessity | Providers, coding |

## Key takeaways

- Most OB/GYN denials come from a handful of repeatable causes.
- Front-end problems can't be fixed by better coding later.
- Work denials quickly — and fix the cause, not just the claim.
- Measure denials by reason and dollars every month.

## A monthly denial review agenda

1. **Totals:** number and dollar value of denials, compared to last month
2. **Top five reasons** by dollars, with trend
3. **By payer:** any payer with a sudden change
4. **By provider:** any provider with an unusual pattern
5. **Fixes in progress:** what was changed last month and whether it worked
6. **New actions:** one or two process changes for the coming month

Thirty minutes a month with this agenda is often enough to cut repeat denials substantially over time.
