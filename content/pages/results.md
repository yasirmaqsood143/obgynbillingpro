## How to read these case studies

The case studies on this page are **illustrative examples**. Each one is a composite drawn from the kinds of situations we've encountered across more than eight years of OB/GYN billing, with identifying details removed and simplified. They show *how we approach* common problems — what we look at, what we fix and what we measure.

They are **not** promises of a specific result. Every practice starts from a different place: payer mix, state Medicaid rules, provider documentation habits, EHR setup and the age of existing A/R all affect what's recoverable and how quickly. The only honest way to know what's possible for your practice is to look at your own data — which is exactly what our free audit does.

## Case study 1: Solo OB/GYN — global maternity billing that had drifted

### The situation

A solo obstetrician-gynecologist with a busy obstetric panel had relied on a general billing company for several years. Collections felt lower than the practice's volume suggested, but the monthly report only showed "claims submitted" and "payments received," so it was impossible to tell why.

### What the audit looked at

- Every delivery over the prior year matched against the pregnancy's antepartum visit count, payer at each visit and delivery type
- Patients who transferred in or out, or changed insurance during pregnancy
- Separately billable services during pregnancy — ultrasounds, non-stress tests, complication visits
- Time from delivery to claim submission

### What we typically find in this situation

- Patients who transferred out after several prenatal visits with **no antepartum-only claim** (`59425` or `59426`) ever submitted
- Global packages billed to the **insurance active at delivery**, even when a different plan covered most of the prenatal care
- **VBAC deliveries billed as routine vaginal deliveries**
- Global claims held for weeks after delivery waiting for the postpartum visit, pushing some close to timely-filing limits

### What we changed

A pregnancy registry from the first visit; eligibility re-verification during pregnancy; a delivery claim checklist (delivery type, VBAC status, `Z37` and `Z3A` codes); and a rule to submit delivery claims promptly according to each payer's policy. Unbilled antepartum care that was still within filing limits was billed.

### What we measured

Unbilled deliveries, open pregnancies by payer, OB claim denial rate and days from delivery to claim submission — reported monthly.

## Case study 2: Four-physician group — a denial backlog that kept growing

### The situation

A four-physician OB/GYN group had two experienced in-house billers who were overwhelmed. Denials were being resubmitted when there was time, A/R over 90 days was climbing, and the same denial reasons appeared month after month.

### What the audit looked at

- Six months of denials, categorized by CARC/RARC code, payer and provider
- A/R aging, with every claim over 90 days checked against timely-filing and appeal deadlines
- Surgical claims compared to operative notes and pathology reports

### What we typically find in this situation

- `CO-97` bundling denials on laparoscopic procedures where modifiers were missing — or where the denial was actually correct and the service shouldn't have been billed
- `CO-197` authorization denials because authorizations didn't match the CPT code actually performed
- `CO-16` rejections from missing data that a claim scrubber rule would have caught
- Aged claims that were still recoverable if appealed promptly — and some that were already past the deadline

### What we changed

We took over denial management and aged A/R so the in-house team could focus on charge entry and patient-facing work. Every denial was categorized; authorization checks were matched to scheduled CPT codes; scrubber rules were added for the most common rejection reasons; and recoverable aged claims were appealed in order of value and deadline.

### What we measured

Denials by reason each month, A/R over 90 days, first-pass acceptance rate and the number of appeals won, lost or pending.

## Case study 3: Multi-location women's health group — first-ever coding audit

### The situation

A women's health group with several locations and a mix of physicians and nurse practitioners had never had an independent coding audit. Leadership wanted to know whether providers were coding consistently and whether any compliance risks existed.

### What the audit looked at

- A random sample of E/M visits per provider, reviewed against MDM and time documentation
- Preventive visits with same-day problem visits and modifier `25`
- LARC insertions and whether device J-codes were billed
- Hysteroscopy and laparoscopy claims against operative notes

### What we typically find in this situation

- E/M levels that varied widely between providers for similar visits — some below what the documentation supported, a few above
- LARC insertions billed **without the device code** when the practice had purchased the device
- Modifier `25` used inconsistently on preventive-plus-problem visits
- Templated notes that didn't clearly document the separately identifiable problem

### What we changed

Provider-level feedback sessions with specific examples; updated documentation templates; a charge-capture check for LARC devices; and a follow-up audit three months later. Where the audit identified overpayments, we documented them and recommended the group involve its compliance advisor in the correction process.

### What we measured

Coding accuracy by provider at baseline and at re-audit, missed-charge rate for LARC devices and modifier `25` consistency.

## The metrics we track for every client

Rather than advertising a single headline number, we report a consistent set of KPIs every month, compared to your own starting point from the free audit:

## What these case studies have in common

Across very different practices, the same lessons repeat:

- **The biggest problems are usually invisible in standard reports.** Unbilled antepartum care, undercoded surgery and underpayments don't show up as denials.
- **Root causes beat resubmissions.** Fixing the eligibility, authorization or documentation step that caused a denial is worth more than any single appeal.
- **Provider feedback matters.** Specific, respectful examples from a provider's own notes change documentation faster than general training.
- **Measurement keeps improvements in place.** Monthly KPIs show whether a fix worked — and catch new problems early.
